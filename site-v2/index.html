Fast medians, percentiles and sigma clipping over stacks of arrays

rankwise reduces one axis of an array to a median, quantile or sigma-clipped mean, for example 40 exposures of the same patch of sky into one image. The results are identical to NumPy, SciPy and astropy, and it runs 15–72× faster on one core. Rust, C and Python.

0 instructions16 pixels per instruction

Each column is one pixel and each row is one exposure of it, loaded into a SIMD register (zmm0–zmm8). Each step is a min and a max instruction on two rows, so all 16 pixels are compared at once. Larger values move down. When the steps finish, the middle row holds the median of every column. The orange values are satellite trails crossing these pixels, and none of them end up in the median.

Why it exists

Stacking telescope exposures, compositing satellite passes and summarizing weather ensembles all reduce a stack of arrays along one axis. Each output pixel is the median or a percentile of a short list, usually 10 to 100 values, and there are millions of pixels.

NumPy, Bottleneck and astropy process those lists one at a time, running a selection algorithm on each. With only 30 or 40 values per list, the fixed cost per list and unpredictable branches dominate, and SIMD barely helps inside a list that short.

rankwise vectorizes across the lists instead, as in the animation above. It sorts 16 pixels per register with a fixed sequence of min/max instructions (a sorting network) and drops the comparisons that can't change the ranks you asked for. The instruction sequence never branches on pixel values.

Examples

Stacking astrophotography exposures

40 synthetic 30-second exposures with satellite trails, an airplane, a meteor and cosmic rays. A plain mean keeps every trail. Sigma clipping drops each pixel's outliers before averaging, with the same decisions as astropy.stats.sigma_clip. Tap the image to see one pixel's 40 values.

clean = rw.sigma_clip_mean(subs, axis=0, sigma=3)

Percentiles of a weather ensemble

One real NOAA GEFS forecast for the contiguous US: 31 ensemble members, 2 m temperature, out to 10 days. The 10th, 50th and 90th percentile at every grid point and lead time (2.5 million lists of 31 values) take 32 ms on one core, against 2.6 s for np.quantile. Height on the map is the p10–p90 spread.

p10, p50, p90 = rw.quantile(members, [0.1, 0.5, 0.9], axis=0)

Cloud-free satellite composites

Every Landsat 8 and 9 pass over Seattle and Tacoma in 2023 and 2024: 90 scenes, with 65% of all pixels under cloud or shadow. Those pixels are stored as 0, and nodata=0 tells rankwise to skip them, so each pixel's median comes only from clear passes. The np.median button shows the same median with the clouds left in.

composite = rw.median(scenes, axis=0, nodata=0)

Usage

The Python functions take NumPy's names and arguments (axis, out, keepdims, method) and work on any axis of a float32, float64 or uint16 array without copying it.
